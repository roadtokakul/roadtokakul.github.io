PMA Kakul — the academy

Where boys arrive and officers leave

On the edge of Abbottabad, where the pine hills of Hazara fold down towards the valley, a bugle sounds before the sun is up. Hundreds of young men are already moving — at the double, in step, in silence. This is the Pakistan Military Academy at Kakul, and every one of them came here to be changed.

How it began

  • The academy was born with the country, in 1947. Its first commandant, Brigadier Francis Ingall, chose Kakul — he knew Abbottabad from summers spent there during his own service.
  • In October 1947 the first cadets arrived from the Indian Military Academy in Dehradun: 67 young men who had chosen Pakistan. They trained in an academy that was still being built around them.
  • Since then the Sword of Honour has been awarded course after course, and Kakul has trained the officers who went on to lead Pakistan's army in every war and every flood, earthquake and rescue since.

The motto

  • “Nasrun minallahi wa fathun qareeb” — help from Allah, and a victory near at hand (Surah As-Saff, 61:13).
  • Cadets learn it in the first days. Its meaning is the academy's whole method: do everything in your power, then trust Allah for the rest.

How the academy is built

  • Cadets live and train in four training battalions, divided into sixteen companies. Your company is your family for two years: you eat, march, compete and suffer together.
  • The companies carry the names of great Muslim commanders and heroes. The first four were Khalid, Tariq, Qasim and Salahuddin. Others include Ghaznavi, Babur, Aurangzeb and Tipu, and Haider, Ubaida, Saad and Hamza. One battalion's companies are named after recipients of the Nishan-e-Haider.
  • Above you stand your platoon commander, your company commander and your battalion commander — serving officers who watch everything and miss almost nothing. The drill and weapon instructors, the ‘ustads’, are senior soldiers whose standards never move.

Who trains here

  • The PMA Long Course is the main road: two years, four terms of six months each, ending with a commission as a Second Lieutenant.
  • Other courses train alongside it at different times — graduate and technical graduate courses, the integrated course for engineering graduates, and the Lady Cadet Course among them. Course names and lengths follow the army's needs, so always check the current advert.
  • Cadets from friendly countries — over the years from places such as Palestine, Iraq, Qatar, Nepal, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka — train side by side with Pakistani cadets. One gold medal at the Passing Out Parade goes to the best of them.

What it is designed to test

  • Body: PT before dawn, runs that get longer and standards that rise every term.
  • Mind: academics and military subjects, under time pressure and on little sleep.
  • Character: whether you tell the truth when a lie would be easier, and whether you carry your share when nobody is watching.
  • Leadership: in the last terms, juniors and coursemates depend on your decisions.

Myths and reality

  • Myth: only the strongest survive. Reality: the most consistent survive. Many star athletes have struggled, and many quiet boys have passed out at the top.
  • Myth: you must be rich or connected. Reality: merit decides — in selection, in training and in the final order of merit.
  • Myth: it is all shouting and punishment. Reality: the pressure is deliberate and controlled. Abuse is not training, and the academy's rules do not allow it.
The bus turns off the main road and climbs. Through the window you see the hills, a mosque's dome, a long white wall. Somebody's father on the seat behind you says quietly, ‘This is it.’ You hold your file a little tighter. In a few minutes a senior will look at your shoes, your hair and your eyes — in that order — and your old life will end.
